Where dependencies come from

Knowing the type tells you which ones you can negotiate.

🔒

Mandatory

Hard logic. Physically or technically required.

Can't test code that doesn't exist.
🤝

Discretionary

Soft logic. A team preference or best practice.

"Design sign-off before build." First to revisit when fast-tracking.
🌐

External

Outside the team's control.

Vendor API keys, another team's service, legal review.
🏠

Internal

Within the project's control.

Your own backend before your own frontend.

When the date is too late

Two classic ways to shorten a schedule. Both only work on the critical path.

💸 Crashing

Backend · 10d (plan) Backend · 7d + 2 devs
What
Add resources to critical tasks: people, overtime, paid tools.
Cost
Money goes up. Pick the cheapest day saved first.
Watch
Diminishing returns. Not every task speeds up with more people.

⏩ Fast-tracking

Backend Frontend
What
Overlap tasks planned in sequence, e.g. build on a mocked API.
Cost
Little extra money, but more risk.
Watch
Rework when the overlapped work changes. Easiest on discretionary links.

Brooks's law

"Adding manpower to a late software project makes it later." Fred Brooks, The Mythical Man-Month (1975).

communication paths: n × (n − 1) ÷ 2
Team size
  • New people need ramp-up time from the people already on the task.
  • Coordination grows much faster than headcount.
  • Adding people works best early, on tasks that split cleanly.

Leveling vs smoothing

One developer is booked 12 hours on days 3 and 4. Two ways to fix it.

Buffers: plan for being wrong

The Critical Chain idea (Eliyahu Goldratt, 1997), in one picture.

  • Take the hidden padding out of each task estimate.
  • Pool it at the end as one project buffer that protects the date.
  • Put a feeding buffer where a side path joins the critical chain.
  • Report how much buffer is used, not just task dates.

🎯 Key takeaways

  • The critical path sets the end date. Float shows where you have slack.
  • Speeding up work off the critical path doesn't move the date.
  • Compress with eyes open: crashing costs money, fast-tracking adds risk.
